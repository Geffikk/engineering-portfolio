A model with its own opinion

A framework for modelling and predicting football events, and then checking whether those predictions would actually have made money. Two different questions, deliberately kept apart, because a model can be right most of the time and still lose.

What it does

Two questions that are easy to confuse

QuestionWho asks itHow the framework answers
Will both teams score in this match? An analyst A trained classifier returns a calibrated probability.
Is betting on that actually profitable? A bettor A financial layer compares that probability against the real bookmaker odds.

Keeping them apart is the whole point. If a model says the favourite wins with 80 percent confidence and the bookmaker offers 1.10, you win often and win almost nothing, and the rare losses eat everything. Accuracy and profitability are not the same measurement, so the framework always runs both layers, one after the other.

Supported events: over / under goals, both teams to score, and 1X2. It runs in two modes: a backtest over historical seasons, and a single-match forecast against live data.

The core discipline

The model never sees the future

The classic mistake in sports modelling is a model that looks brilliant on paper because it quietly learned from results it should not have known yet. The whole evaluation is built as a rolling, walk-forward backtest so that cannot happen.

1 · TRAIN

Past matches only

The model is fitted on fixtures played strictly before the window being predicted.

2 · PREDICT

One 84-hour window

Roughly a matchday of fixtures is scored at once, with no knowledge of any result inside it.

3 · SCORE

Against real results and real odds

Both layers run: was the prediction right, and would the bet have paid.

4 · ADVANCE

Fold in and refit

The scored window joins the training set, the window moves forward and the model is retrained.

It is re-sitting an exam every week where you may only study last week’s material. Slower than a single train/test split, and the only way the resulting ROI number means anything.

Architecture

Sources, features, models, money

The most interesting idea

A loss function that penalises agreeing with the bookmaker

A bettor only profits where they disagree with the market and turn out to be right. A model that perfectly reproduces the bookmaker’s opinion has, by construction, zero edge: it just pays the margin. So the flagship model does not only optimise for being correct.

The trap

Train a classifier on football data with the bookmaker’s implied probability available anywhere in the feature set, or even just correlated with it, and it converges on the market price. It scores well on accuracy and it is worthless as a betting signal.

What I built

A custom Keras loss with two terms: ordinary binary cross-entropy for be correct, plus a decorrelation penalty for be different from the bookmaker. The model is explicitly pushed to find its own signal instead of rediscovering the market price.

Why it is honest

Because it is measured against an always-back-the-favourite baseline that is the market. If the decorrelated model cannot beat that, the penalty bought nothing, and the comparison says so instead of hiding it.

Feature engineering

Every column is readable from its name

st_diff_shots_on_goal_ha_ma_g  →  short term · home minus away · shots on goal · home/away split · moving average · goals-based

ELO and form

Chess-style strength rating updated after every match, plus recent results and accumulated points.

Expected goals

Chance quality rather than luck, taken from two independent providers and averaged over short and long horizons.

Match statistics

Moving averages of shots, corners and possession, split by home and away because the split carries real signal.

Head to head

History between these two specific teams, which sometimes disagrees with their general form.

Team power

Separate defence, midfield and attack ratings derived from published squad ratings.

Context

Years in the top league, competition, date, and match-day weather resolved from the stadium location.

Not every historical match deserves equal influence. Three optional weighting schemes feed the loss function directly: recency scales the weight linearly from 1 for the oldest match to 4 for the newest, because a 2012 fixture is a weaker guide to today than a 2024 one; odds weighting leans on the market’s own confidence; and underdog weighting boosts the matches where the favourite lost, which are exactly the ones a profitable model has to get right.

Labels come from expected goals, not only from actual goals. Expected goals are continuous, so 1.76 to 1.51 is nominally a home win but rounds to a 2-2 draw. An entropy margin keeps those near-ties out of the confident class, so the model is never trained on coin flips presented as certainties. Missing values are handled by an explicit strategy per model family, including a sentinel value that tree models can split on rather than a mean that quietly invents an average team.

Evaluation

Baselines are what make a number a claim

“62 percent accuracy” means nothing on its own. “62 percent, where random gets 50 and always backing the favourite gets 58” is a claim. Three baselines ship alongside the three real models for exactly that reason: a random floor, a naive historical base rate, and the market itself.

Expected profit

For probability p and decimal odds o, the edge per unit staked is p × o − 1. A configurable threshold decides how much edge is worth a bet at all.

Bet selection

Most confident prediction, highest expected profit, or a majority vote across events followed by highest expected profit.

Stake sizing

Flat stakes or the Kelly criterion. Kelly maximises long-run growth and is volatile; flat staking is calmer and makes backtests comparable.

Calibration over accuracy

Brier score is reported because expected profit is computed directly from the predicted probability. A model that says 70 percent had better be right 70 percent of the time.

Honest limitations

What this project is not

It is a research framework, not a product, and its README says so in a section written for whoever picks it up next. There is no test suite. The dataset is not distributed, only the paths to it. Forecast mode is fully implemented but reachable only by editing a constant. The scrapers depend on markup that four different sites change whenever they feel like it.

Documenting that is deliberate. A limitations section costs nothing to write and saves the next person an afternoon, and a project whose weaknesses are unstated is a project nobody can trust the strengths of.
