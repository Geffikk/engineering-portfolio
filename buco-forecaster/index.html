Overview

The problem, and what I built

Predicting football results with machine learning is a well-worn exercise, and most of it is worthless for two reasons. The model quietly learns from matches it should not have known about yet, so the reported accuracy is fiction. And even when the accuracy is real, nobody checks whether acting on it would have made or lost money.

The second point is the one people skip. If a model says the favourite wins with 80 percent confidence and the bookmaker offers odds of 1.10, you are right most of the time, win almost nothing, and the rare losses eat everything. Being accurate and being profitable are simply not the same measurement.

So the framework does both, in that order. It gathers years of match data from six sources, engineers a large feature set out of it, trains several models to predict a specific event, and then hands every prediction to a second layer that compares it against the real bookmaker odds of that day and decides whether the bet was worth placing at all.

It predicts three kinds of event: over or under a number of goals, whether both teams score, and home win, draw or away win. It runs either as a historical backtest across whole seasons or as a single prediction for one upcoming match. It was built as a university thesis and it is a research framework rather than a product.

How it works

From years of matches to one honest number

The whole evaluation is a rolling backtest. The model is retrained over and over as the window moves forward, which is slow, and the only way the resulting profit figure means anything.

1 · GATHER

Six sources into one table

Fixtures, statistics and odds from an API; chance quality, historical odds and squad ratings scraped. The output is one row per match.

2 · BUILD

Engineer the features

Strength ratings, recent form, chance quality, in-match statistics, head-to-head history, squad power and context such as weather.

3 · TRAIN

On the past only

The model sees matches played strictly before the round it is about to predict. Nothing later exists as far as it is concerned.

4 · PREDICT

One round of fixtures

Roughly a matchday is predicted at once, with no knowledge of any result inside that window.

5 · SCORE

Against reality and against the odds

Both layers run: was the prediction right, and would the bet have paid at the real prices of that day.

6 · ADVANCE

Move forward and retrain

The scored round joins the training data, the window shifts, and the model is trained again from scratch.

It is re-sitting an exam every week where you are only ever allowed to study last week’s material. You never accidentally revise tomorrow’s answers, which is exactly the mistake that makes so many published sports models look brilliant and behave badly.

Architecture

Sources, features, models, money

Six external sources on the left, the feature pipeline in the middle, the models and the money layer on the right. The dashed line back is the retraining loop.

The unglamorous half is getting the data to line up. Seven leagues across five independent sources, none of which share an identifier and none of which spell team names the same way. Every source carries its own alias table and fuzzy matching covers the rest. It is the same problem I ran into years later on the arbitrage platform, and it is still the part that decides whether everything above it works.

Everything else is configuration. Which models, which features, how matches are weighted, what counts as enough edge to bet, how much to stake: all of it lives in a config file, so any experiment can be reproduced from a diff rather than from memory. That is what makes it a framework instead of a script.

Engineering

Two ideas worth explaining

The main idea

A model trained to disagree with the bookmaker

You only make money where you disagree with the market and turn out to be right. A model that reproduces the bookmaker’s opinion has no edge at all by construction: it just pays the margin. And that is exactly what a model trained on football data tends to converge on, because the bookmaker is already very good.

So the main model is trained on two goals at once. The usual one, be correct, plus a penalty that grows the closer its prediction sits to the market price. It is a custom loss function, and it pushes the model to find its own signal instead of rediscovering the odds. Whether it worked is measured against a baseline that is the market, so the comparison cannot flatter itself.

Labelling

Football scores lie, so the labels come from chances

A team can dominate a match and lose 1-0. Training on the scoreline teaches the model that the better performance was the worse one, which is noise dressed up as signal. Labels are therefore built from expected goals, which measure the quality of chances created rather than whether they went in.

That introduces a second problem: expected goals are continuous, so a match at 1.76 to 1.51 rounds to a home win while really being a coin flip. A margin keeps those near-ties out of the confident class entirely, so the model is never trained on coin flips presented as certainties.

Results

What it produces, and what it is not

The output is a cumulative profit curve per league and per configuration, accuracy and calibration tables, and stored result objects so several experiments can be replotted on one chart. Calibration matters more than accuracy here, because the profit is computed straight from the predicted probability: a model that says 70 percent had better be right about 70 percent of the time.

Three baselines run alongside every experiment: random guessing, the historical base rate, and always backing the favourite. “62 percent accuracy” means nothing on its own. “62 percent, where random gets 50 and the market gets 58” is a claim.

What it is not. It is a research framework, not a product, and the repository says so in a section written for whoever picks it up next. There is no test suite. The dataset is not distributed, only the paths to it. Live forecast mode is fully implemented but reachable only by editing a constant. The scrapers depend on markup that four different websites change whenever they feel like it.

Writing that down was deliberate. A limitations section costs nothing and saves the next person an afternoon, and a project whose weaknesses are unstated is one whose strengths cannot be trusted either.
