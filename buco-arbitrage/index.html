Overview

The problem, and what I built

Two venues can disagree about the same football or tennis match. A bookmaker offers odds on one team winning, and a prediction-market exchange sells shares that pay out if the other team wins. When those prices disagree far enough, buying both sides costs less than the payout, and it stops mattering who wins.

The catch is that the window lasts seconds and only pays if both purchases succeed. Doing that by hand is not realistic, and the obvious naive version of it, a script that checks a price and then buys, is exactly how people end up holding one side of a trade they never wanted.

So the system does the whole job on its own: it watches both venues continuously, works out which listings describe the same real match, prices the opportunity, decides whether it is worth taking, buys both sides, and then proves what actually happened from what the venues really filled.

It has run in production since early 2026 on a single self-hosted Linux server, trading real money inside limits that live in the code rather than in my head. I designed, built and operate all of it, down to the deploy pipeline and the alerting.

How it works

One opportunity, from price to settlement

Six steps. The first four are free to abandon, the last two are not, and almost every design decision in the project comes from that one difference.

1 · WATCH

Collect prices

Both venues stream live prices over WebSocket. Where a venue has no usable API at all, a headless browser reads the page instead.

2 · MATCH

Pair the event

The venues share no identifiers and spell team names differently, so pairing them is its own problem, solved with fuzzy matching over a hand-curated alias list.

3 · PRICE

Find the gap

If the two opposite outcomes together cost less than they pay out, that gap is the profit. Below a minimum gap nothing happens at all.

4 · CHECK

Decide whether to take it

Prices are re-read, fees, currency conversion and slippage are subtracted, and the spending limits are applied. Most opportunities die right here.

5 · BUY

Place both sides

The bookmaker side goes through an automated browser session, the exchange side through its order book. This is the point of no return.

6 · PROVE

Record what happened

Both purchases are reconciled against what the venues actually filled, and the trade closes with a named outcome and a real profit figure.

Step five is the uncomfortable one. The two venues are unrelated companies, so there is no way to buy both sides as a single transaction and no way to undo one of them. If the first purchase succeeds and the second is rejected, the system is holding a one-sided bet with real money in it, at three in the morning, with nobody watching. Most of what follows on this page exists to make that rare, and survivable when it happens anyway.

Architecture

Twenty-seven containers, four kinds of job

Collect prices, decide, buy, and show the operator what happened. Everything else is a variation on one of those four.

Collecting is split by volume. The exchange order book arrives as a firehose of updates, so those services are written in Go, where the latency and memory behaviour are predictable. Everything else, including the browser-driven scrapers, is Python, because that side of the system changes every week.

Deciding is one service. It re-reads the prices, sizes the trade, applies every limit and orders the two purchases. Buying is one service per venue, so a venue having a bad day cannot stall the other one.

There are two separate databases on purpose. Live prices go into a store that can be thrown away and rebuilt from the venues at any moment. Anything about money in flight goes into a different, durable store with a replica and backups.

Clearing a cache of stale prices is a routine thing to do. Clearing the record of which trades are half-finished is an incident. Keeping them physically apart is what stops the routine action from ever becoming the incident. A third database holds the history, the event pairings and the audit trail, and nothing that decides about money is ever allowed to read from it.

Deployment

How it ships and how it is run

Merging to the main branch is the deployment. The orchestrator watches the repository, pulls the new commit and recreates whatever changed. There is no deploy script and nothing on the server is edited by hand.

The same compose file is both the build instruction and the deployment, so what was tested is what runs. Passwords and keys live in the orchestrator rather than the repository, and the stack refuses to start when one is missing, so a half-configured deploy fails loudly instead of quietly running degraded. Rolling back is a revert and another poll.

The one real cost: a deploy recreates every container, so a trade caught in that window fails. New work therefore stops being accepted before a rollout starts.

Monitoring runs as its own separate stack, because a monitor that dies together with the thing it monitors is decoration. Server and container metrics, live logs from every container in one place, and error tracking fed by all the services.

On top of that sits a watchdog whose only job is to notice absence: that something which should be running is not running at all. Ordinary alerting only reacts to things that happen, such as a crash, and a cleanly stopped stack produces no event whatsoever. So the watchdog works from a written list of what is meant to exist, rather than from whatever happens to be running.

Alerts go to chat with quiet periods, so a normal deploy raises nothing, and a daily heartbeat, so that silence itself can be trusted.

Engineering

Three problems worth describing

Atomicity

Two processes can both be told the seat is free

Deciding and then acting are two separate moments, and anything can happen in between. Two processes ask the database whether a trade is already running for a match, both are told no, and both start one. The window is a couple of hundred milliseconds, and a restart at the wrong instant is enough to hit it.

The fix was to stop asking. The whole decision now runs inside the database as a single script that checks and writes without anything interleaving, because that database executes one script at a time. Nobody looks through the window and then walks in; you hand your ticket to the one person allowed to seat anyone, and they handle tickets one at a time.

Proof

Deciding to send an order is not the same as sending it

An early version recorded that it had hedged a position and then died before the order actually left the process. The database said covered. Reality said exposed. Nothing was lying; the two facts had simply never been written together.

Now “an order was decided” and “an order was emitted” are two separate recorded facts written in the same atomic step, and a scheduled audit checks that they always agree. The same principle runs through the money: every figure is computed from what the venue actually filled, never from what the system intended to buy.

Rollout

A new safety rule watches before it acts

A rule that blocks trades and is subtly wrong does not look like a bug. It looks like a quiet week. Nothing turns red, there are simply fewer trades, and by the time anyone wonders why, the evidence is weeks old.

So every new rule first runs in a mode where it records what it would have blocked, without blocking anything. After a few days of that, once the numbers make sense against what I expected, it gets permission to act. It is the same reason you would have a new doorman write down who he would have turned away before you let him turn anyone away.

Results

What it produces, and what it refuses to do

Every trade ends with a name rather than a pass or fail: completed, closed early at a known small loss, one-sided and needing attention, or refused by a specific rule. Each carries the evidence behind it, which is what makes the daily summary readable and recovery automatable.

Open exposure has not been engineered away, because it cannot be. A venue can always go down mid-trade. What changed is that every occurrence is detected immediately, names which side is uncovered, and can be attributed afterwards to a venue outage or to my own bug.

The refusals are the part I would defend hardest. Automatic trading never knowingly accepts a losing worst case, even when the average looks good, because the average is not what gets paid out. Stale prices stop the trade rather than being used anyway. A great price with almost no volume behind it is not treated as a price. Spending is capped per trade and again per match, so one event cannot absorb everything through repeated attempts.

And every venue has an off switch plus a simulated mode that follows exactly the same code path without spending anything, which is how anything new gets proven before it is trusted.
